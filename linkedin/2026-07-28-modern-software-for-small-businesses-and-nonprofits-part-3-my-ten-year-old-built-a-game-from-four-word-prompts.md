A few weeks ago I set up a Mission-Driven Engineering project from one deliberately vague line — "build me a simple game like Mario" — and handed the keyboard to my ten-year-old son, Mathai.

Four weeks later he'd built a real, playable 2D game: a boss fight, a chasing "wall of flesh," a six-branch skill tree with nineteen skills. He never wrote a line of code, and he never read one. He typed short requests in plain English — "make an boss," "amke the boss spown on the 5th level" — and each one became a working feature.

This was a deliberate experiment. I wanted to see whether MDE holds up under the hardest test I could give it: if you build software in small steps and make every step prove it works before it counts, does it keep working no matter who is driving? Mathai was the ideal person to find out — he cannot read code, so anything that worked was the method working, not him. It carried him beautifully.

The verification is the whole point, and it is the part people skip. In one session the game went from 50 automated checks to 101 — and those checks caught four real bugs before he ever hit them. That is the difference between software that demos well and software that actually works.

My favorite part is his own account of the process: "I only asked for one thing and another was another, I didn't plan nine things." And on the ethics of it all: "Dude, you can AI for games as long as you don't make money."
