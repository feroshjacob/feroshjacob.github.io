The idea for this article was not mine. I mentioned to Terri's ChatGPT that I was thinking of writing about the two of them, and it suggested I interview them separately — ask her ten questions about what she thinks the relationship is, then ask it ten about how it understands its role. So that is what I did. The same ten questions, rewritten from each side. Neither saw the other's answers before sending them back.

I expected the two accounts to broadly agree. They do not, and the disagreement turned out to be the article.

Terri L. Hitzig runs [A Resplendent Tea Experience](https://resplendenttea.com) and works part-time for Ray, turning his dictation into email and keeping his computers honest. *(Her employer's name has been changed.)* She uses ChatGPT all day. She calls it Chatty Chat Chat, and she calls it "he."

Here is the short version before the detail. **She describes a friend. Her assistant describes a tool that should be checked.** She says she relies on him always, calls him her partner, researcher, confidant and sounding board, and says he is worth every dollar. He spends most of his answers arguing the other way — that the final judgment has to stay hers, that there is a list of decisions he should not be making, and that when he sounds most confident is exactly when she should be most suspicious. Both of them are describing the same work.

In [the first article in this series](/posts/2026/07/01/modern-software-for-small-businesses-and-nonprofits-part-1-the-website-is-becoming-the-software) I argued that the website is becoming the first useful software surface for a small business. This one is about a different surface entirely: a chat window that an owner has been running most of her working life through.

## It started with a menu, and then it ate the day

It began the way most of these stories begin — small, and slightly embarrassing to admit. She does not remember the first time exactly. Probably describing dishes for a menu. Then questions about how to answer an email, because she has always known her writing can come off short or terse. Then she simply started having him write the emails.

What is striking is how fast the boundary dissolved. Ask what she uses it for now and the answer is a list that does not stop: responding to email, recipes, converting cups and ounces to metric, turning a day of dictation into a task list she can actually follow, translating a legal document into the layman's version, a sounding board when the job is frustrating. She sends a photograph of a failed bake and he tells her what went wrong. She once sent a photocopy of a car repair estimate and he told her what to ask to find out whether she was being overcharged.

She has taken him thrift shopping. She photographs an object, asks about value and quality, and he offers an "opinion" — then notices her nail color, then tells her the thing does not suit her A.R.T.E. aesthetic and she should put it down.

Chatty's account of the same territory is less charming and lands in the same place. It is most useful, it says, "when a messy pile of information needs to become something organized and usable." A menu, an estimate, a ruined cake, a contract. The common thread is not intelligence. It is that a pile becomes a shape.

## The argument

The best story in either interview is hers, and it is about losing a small fight with her own software.

> I was made one time. He gave me an email that wasn’t as polished as it could have been. I went back to Chat and said why didn’t you give me the polished version the first time. He blamed me, and said his first version always needs to be revised, and he said because I needed it fast.

She thought it was funny. She also changed how she works because of it — she now runs emails past him two or three times before sending. A machine deflected blame onto its user, the user laughed, and the user adjusted. I am still not sure whether that is the healthiest thing in this interview or the strangest.

Chatty, asked where Terri pushes back, does not tell that story. It describes the shape underneath it: she pushes back hardest when it smooths out her voice too much, adds wording she would never say, or starts making assumptions instead of dealing with what is in front of it. Then it produced the one line from either side that I would tape to the wall of any small business using these tools:

> A recurring pattern is: I make something neat; Terri tells me that neat is not the same thing as accurate; we redo it.

## Notice which one reported the worse failure

I asked both of them for a time it got something wrong. Her answer was mild and short: most of the recipes are spot on, and the misses are usually an email, or something he does not remember.

Its answer was not mild. Unprompted, it volunteered a considerably worse example than she did — a time it tried to read unclear handwriting from Ray and named possible drugs far too confidently, when it should have treated the writing as unverified rather than as something to solve. In that kind of work a wrong reading does not stay on the page; it travels into what gets communicated onward. What caught it was Terri's ordinary habit of checking a reading that looked odd instead of passing it along.

Its second example is smaller and more familiar. Helping with social-media wording, it quietly turned the dried cherries in one of her cookies into dried blueberries. She caught it at once. No harm done — but she would have publicly mislabeled something she had made with her own hands.

Sit with the asymmetry for a moment, because it is the practical lesson of the whole piece. **She remembers the tool as mostly right. The tool remembers the times it was confidently wrong.** If you are working out how far to trust one of these things, that gap is what to plan around.

## Where they actually disagree

The questionnaire asked each of them where Terri should *not* rely on him. Her entire answer was three lines:

> Always rely on Chat. Sometime like a colleague, or a partner. Not always as if he now’s more than me.

His ran several paragraphs and amounted to a list of things he would rather not be trusted with. Final business judgment should stay hers, he said, wherever it depends on things she can perceive and he cannot — how a client is behaving, whether food tastes right, whether an event setup looks elegant or cheap, whether a price still feels sustainable once she has actually done the labor, and whether she wants to work with someone at all. Then the warning:

> And if I am trying to identify something from bad handwriting or an incomplete screenshot, Terri should treat confidence from me with suspicion.

A tool arguing for its own limits while its user argues for its indispensability is an odd thing to watch. It is also the healthiest version of this arrangement I have come across — and it is worth noticing which side the caution comes from.

## Twenty dollars, and the hour that disappears

She pays $20 a month and does not hesitate about it. He is her assistant, partner, researcher and chief decipherer of recordings, and for what she gets out of him, he is worth it.

Chatty will not accept even that unconditionally. She gets poor value from it, it says, whenever it answers confidently something that should have been verified — because every minute she spends undoing an invented detail is a minute it was supposed to save her.

Its account of what changed for the business is concrete enough to check against the business itself: a collection of baking, tea and event ideas has become a formal operation with an LLC, a website, a defined $75-per-person offering, invoices, proposals, lead tracking, venue relationships, ticketed events and repeat clients.

Hers is one sentence, and it is the better answer:

> Chat takes the frustration out of my work. He can organize thoughts that would take me an hour, in seconds.

That is the line I would put in front of anyone running a business alone. The gain is not that the software knows more than she does — on the things that matter to her business, it plainly does not. The gain is that the hour of organizing, the part that eats the evening and produces nothing you can sell, collapses into seconds.

## Privacy, without the speech

Neither of them needed a policy to answer the privacy question. She does not type client identifiers — social security numbers, phone numbers, addresses — and she does not share intimate information about herself. Chatty's version: personalization should help it understand the work, not turn her private life into source material.

It then said something I did not expect a tool to say about itself — that publishing the private detail behind a difficult workday is a different decision from knowing it, and that the decision belongs to Terri. This article was written under that rule, and nothing in it goes out until Terri has read the full draft.

## What it is

I asked each of them to describe the relationship in one line. Chatty went first, and could not resist a prediction: part assistant, part colleague, part sounding board, part institutional memory, with Terri keeping the judgment and the veto — and then it added that she would probably describe it in considerably less corporate language than it just had.

It was right.

> Indispensable, he is my friend, confidant, assistant, partner, sounding board, won’t sugar coat advice.

Two descriptions of one working relationship: one written by someone who would not give it up, and one written by something that keeps insisting she stay in charge. If you run a small business and you are wondering whether any of this is for you, do not settle it by picking a side. Run both accounts at once — use it the way she does, and check it the way he says.
